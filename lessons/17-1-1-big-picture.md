## Six things that make it sound Brazilian

Brazilian Portuguese has a few features that you'll hear in almost every sentence. Learn to notice these first; everything else in this chapter fills in the details.

| What happens | Example |
|---|---|
| Unstressed final *-o* sounds like /u/ (and final *-e* like /i/) | *tudo*<br><span class="ipa">/ˈtu.du/</span><br>everything |
| *t* and *d* before an /i/ sound become /t͡ʃ/ and /d͡ʒ/ ("chee", "jee") | *noite*<br><span class="ipa">/ˈnoj.t͡ʃi/</span><br>night |
| Vowels can be nasal (air through the nose) | *pão*<br><span class="ipa">/ˈpɐ̃w̃/</span><br>bread |
| *l* at the end of a syllable sounds like /w/ | *Brasil*<br><span class="ipa">/bɾaˈziw/</span><br>Brazil |
| A single *r* between vowels is a quick tap… | *caro*<br><span class="ipa">/ˈka.ɾu/</span><br>expensive |
| …while *rr* (and *r* at the start of a word) is a strong, breathy r | *carro*<br><span class="ipa">/ˈka.ʁu/</span><br>car |
| *e* and *o* can be open (*é*, *ó*)… | *avó*<br><span class="ipa">/aˈvɔ/</span><br>grandmother |
| …or closed (*ê*, *ô*), and this can change the word | *avô*<br><span class="ipa">/aˈvo/</span><br>grandfather |

The *noite* example shows two features at once: the final *-e* becomes /i/, and that /i/ turns the *t* before it into /t͡ʃ/. That's why *leite* /ˈlej.t͡ʃi/ ("milk") ends in something like "chee."

## Which accent this book uses

Brazil is big, and accents vary. This book follows the general Brazilian pronunciation given in Wiktionary, which is close to the neutral south-eastern accent of São Paulo and Minas Gerais — the accent most textbooks and phonetic descriptions use as their model. Part 17-6 covers the main regional differences.

## Dictionary vs. real speech

The IPA in this book is the **dictionary form** of each word, strung together. In real conversation, words run into each other and common words shrink:

- *Você está com fome?* <span class="ipa">/voˈse isˈta kõ ˈfõ.mi/</span> — Are you hungry? (careful)
- *Cê tá com fome?* <span class="ipa">/ˈse ˈta kõ ˈfõ.mi/</span> — Are you hungry? (everyday)

The audio buttons show this difference directly: **📖 Textbook** reads every word fully and separately, while **▶ Natural** is how a Brazilian would actually say the sentence. Listen to both — the gap between them is what Part 17-5 (Connected Speech) is about.

## Watch out

> **Spelling is not sound.** Don't read Portuguese letter by letter as you would romaji: *leite* is not "le-i-te," and *noite* is not "no-i-te." Final *-e* and *-o* are almost always /i/ and /u/ in Brazil.

> **You'll hear other accents.** In Rio de Janeiro, an *s* at the end of a syllable sounds like /ʃ/; in the far South, a final *l* can stay /l/; in parts of the Northeast, *t* and *d* before /i/ stay /t/ and /d/, and unstressed vowels sound more open. None of these is wrong.

> **Don't wait to be perfect.** These features become automatic with listening. Keep going with the grammar chapters and come back to this chapter whenever a sound puzzles you.

## Quick check

<details markdown="1">
<summary>1. How does the end of <em>noite</em> ("night") sound?</summary>

Like "chee": the final *-e* is /i/, and it turns the *t* into /t͡ʃ/ — *noite* /ˈnoj.t͡ʃi/.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>2. What sound does the final <em>l</em> in <em>Brasil</em> make?</summary>

/w/ — *Brasil* /bɾaˈziw/, like English "w" at the end of a word.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>3. What's the difference between <em>caro</em> and <em>carro</em>?</summary>

*caro* /ˈka.ɾu/ ("expensive") has a quick tap; *carro* /ˈka.ʁu/ ("car") has the strong r.
</details>
